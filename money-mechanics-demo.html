How compounding works

You put in £100, shown as 100 £1 coins. Once a year, every coin you own earns interest, including coins that were themselves earned as interest.

  • ● Grey: your original 100 coins. They never change.
  • ● Gold: this year's interest, always the top row.
  • ● Blue: interest from earlier years. Once a year is over, its gold coins turn blue because they've joined your pot and start earning too.
  • Gold coins with a blue ring were earned by the blue coins. That's interest earning interest: compounding.

Each year's new coins get their own row, stacked on top of the year before. As the blue pile grows, it earns more ringed coins each year, so every new gold row is longer than the last. Coins keep one fixed size and the stack scrolls up as it grows, so nothing ever shrinks. Coins are rounded to whole pounds; the sum below uses exact pennies.

Formula: total = £100 × (1 + rate)years, with growth added once a year.

Savings vs investments

  • Savings: the bank pays interest. It compounds when you leave the interest in the account, so next year's interest is paid on a bigger balance.
  • Investments (shares, funds): there's no interest. Instead, the value of what you own goes up or down. A rise of 10% applies to the whole value, including past gains, so as long as you stay invested your gains make gains. That's compounding.
  • Dividends are cash some investments pay out. Reinvesting them (accumulation funds do this automatically) keeps them compounding; taking them as cash means that part stops growing.
  • Real returns are bumpy. Markets don't rise the same amount every year, and some years fall. The steady rate here is a simplified average, not a prediction.
  • Losses compound too. A 50% fall needs a 100% rise just to get back to where you started. Fees work the same way: a yearly fee is taken from a growing pot, so its cost grows over time.

The rates are examples only. Real investments rise and fall, returns are not steady each year, and fees, tax and inflation reduce what you keep. This is a learning tool, not financial advice.

How the goal planner works

It works backwards in three steps, all in today's money, so £25,000 means what £25,000 buys now. You set growth before inflation and the inflation rate; the planner converts growth to after inflation.

  • Spending goal: what you want to spend each year, after tax. The planner works out the tax for you.
  • From your pot: what you'd need to take out each year, on top of your pensions and income, so that after tax you can spend your goal. The full new State Pension is £241.30 a week (about £12,548 a year). Many people get less, depending on their National Insurance record, so check your forecast at GOV.UK.
  • Pot needed (Keep a cushion): the gap divided by how much you take out each year. Taking 4% is the well-known "4% rule", which comes from research using US market history, so 3.5% is used here as a more cautious default. No withdrawal rate is guaranteed to last. If you retire before State Pension age, the pot also covers the missing State Pension for those years.
  • Pot needed (Spend it all by an age): just enough to cover every year's shortfall from retirement to the age you choose, with the rest growing at your retirement growth rate. Before your State Pension or final salary pension starts you take out more, and afterwards less. If you live past that age, the pot is empty.
  • Monthly saving: what you'd need to save each month from now, on top of what you've already saved, assuming steady growth and monthly compounding.

Lifestyle presets use the PLSA Retirement Living Standards for one person: about £13,900 a year (Minimum), £32,700 (Moderate) and £45,400 (Comfortable). These are spending figures, not income, and they assume you own your home outright, so add rent or mortgage costs if you'll still have them.

Bills: add up what you expect to spend each month in retirement, in today's prices. Housing often falls if a mortgage is paid off, while health, care and home repairs can rise with age. The example figures are only placeholders. For realistic yearly budgets, see the PLSA Retirement Living Standards.

Tax: you only need to say what you want to spend; the planner estimates income tax using 2026/27 rates for England, Wales and Northern Ireland (a £12,570 Personal Allowance, 20% up to £50,270, 40% up to £125,140 and 45% above, with the allowance reduced above £100,000).

  • State Pension, final salary pensions and other income count as taxable income.
  • Pension pots: usually 25% can be taken tax-free, up to a £268,275 lump sum allowance; the rest is taxed as income. The planner assumes you take 25% of each withdrawal tax-free.
  • ISAs: money taken out of an ISA isn't taxed.
  • Pension vs ISA: pensions usually get tax relief and employer contributions going in, and ISAs don't, so paying less tax later doesn't make an ISA the better choice. You can put up to £20,000 a year into ISAs (2026/27).

This is a simplified estimate. It doesn't cover Scottish tax rates, tax on savings outside ISAs and pensions, or changes to tax rules, which happen often.

Other pensions and income:

  • Other pots (old workplace pensions, personal pensions/SIPPs, ISAs) are added to what you've already saved. The government's free Pension Tracing Service can help find old workplace pensions.
  • Paying in now counts what already goes in each month from you and your employer, so the headline shows only the extra needed.
  • Final salary (defined benefit) pensions pay a set yearly income from a set age. Enter the amount in today's money, from your latest pension statement. Many rise with inflation each year, often up to a cap; if yours doesn't, its real value will fall over time and this planner will overstate it.
  • Other income such as rent or part-time work is treated as steady for the whole of retirement, which may not be realistic.
  • If a pension starts after you retire, the pot also covers it until it starts. This bridge is a simplified, rough amount.

Your State Pension age depends on your date of birth. It's rising from 66 to 67 and may rise further, so check yours at GOV.UK.

Paying into a pension can reduce your cost: with basic-rate tax relief, £80 from you becomes £100 in the pension. Employer contributions also count towards the monthly figure.

Real returns go up and down and are never guaranteed, which is why the range matters more than any single number. This is an educational tool, not financial advice. For a personal plan, speak to a regulated financial adviser, or use free guidance from MoneyHelper.

Will it last?

This takes the pot from your plan and plays retirement forward one year at a time, in today's money.

  • Each year you take out what you need minus the pensions and income being paid that year. Before your State Pension or final salary pension starts, you take out more; once they start, you take out less.
  • What's left grows at the rate you choose with the slider (0–12% a year). The planner takes off the inflation rate you set in Plan it, because everything is in today's money: if investments grow 8% in a year when prices rise 3%, that's about 5% after inflation. For reference, world shares returned about 5.2% a year after inflation from 1900 to 2024, and bonds about 1.7% (UBS Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2025).
  • Real returns go up and down, and a run of bad years early in retirement can make money run out sooner than an average suggests.
  • It runs to age 100 (or later if you chose to spend it all by a later age). Many people live into their 90s, so it's worth planning for a long retirement.
  • It doesn't include tax on withdrawals beyond what your income goal already allows for, care costs, or one-off spending.
